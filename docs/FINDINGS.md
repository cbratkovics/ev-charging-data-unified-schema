## 1. Boulder: idle-after-charge time is large; about a quarter of it blocks another driver

**Period.** Boulder, 2018-01-01 to 2023-11-30, non-trivial sessions at known stations.

**What was found.** Over 14,742,434 connected minutes, 6,643,229 were idle after charging ended: an idle share of 45.1%. <!-- cite: artifacts/findings/latest.json#boulder_idle.production.connected_minutes; artifacts/findings/latest.json#boulder_idle.production.idle_minutes; artifacts/findings/latest.json#boulder_idle.production.idle_share_of_connected --> Of those idle minutes, 1,834,372 elapsed while every inferred port at the station was occupied: a blocking idle share of 12.4% of connected time, or 27.6% of idle time, and every one of the 50 stations has some. <!-- cite: artifacts/findings/latest.json#boulder_idle.production.blocking_idle_minutes; artifacts/findings/latest.json#boulder_idle.production.blocking_idle_share_of_connected; artifacts/findings/latest.json#boulder_idle.production.blocking_share_of_idle; artifacts/findings/latest.json#boulder_idle.production.stations --> The blocking share of connected time ranges from 11.6% to 12.5% across the robust-max port definitions (N = 1 to 10). <!-- cite: artifacts/findings/latest.json#boulder_idle.robust_max_n1.blocking_idle_share_of_connected; artifacts/findings/latest.json#boulder_idle.robust_max_n10.blocking_idle_share_of_connected; artifacts/findings/latest.json#boulder_idle.robust_max_n2.blocking_idle_share_of_connected; artifacts/findings/latest.json#boulder_idle.robust_max_n3.blocking_idle_share_of_connected; artifacts/findings/latest.json#boulder_idle.robust_max_n5.blocking_idle_share_of_connected --> Blocking idle is a midday phenomenon: local hours 11, 12, 10 carry the most of it. <!-- cite: artifacts/findings/latest.json#boulder_idle_by_hour_production[11].hour; artifacts/findings/latest.json#boulder_idle_by_hour_production[12].hour; artifacts/findings/latest.json#boulder_idle_by_hour_production[10].hour -->

**Why it matters.** The headline idle share is the number that usually gets quoted as recoverable capacity. On this data it overstates the part a policy could recover by about four times: most idle minutes happen while another port at the same station is free, so no driver was blocked by them. The blocking part is still substantial and happens at every station, at midday, when demand peaks.

**What I would tell the decision-maker.** Lead with the blocking share. An idle fee or a time limit could recover at most the blocking minutes, and they cluster between late morning and mid-afternoon; a time-of-day rule would target most of the recoverable minutes without touching overnight idling, which blocks nobody.

**What would change my mind.** Evidence that ports are undercounted at the busy stations would lower the blocking share (the sensitivity range shows how far); evidence of queues or turned-away drivers (not in the data) would raise the value of each blocking minute. Charging is assumed to begin at session start; if drivers plug in and charging starts later, some 'idle' minutes were charging.

## 2. UK DfT 2017: the published exclusion rule does not describe the published exclusions

**Period.** Calendar 2017, one year, one funding programme.

**What was found.** The publisher states one exclusion rule: zero energy or a plug-in of three minutes or less. Applying it to the accepted rows of the rapids anomalies file marks 20,699 of 38,376 rows; the rest do not meet the stated rule. <!-- cite: artifacts/findings/latest.json#dft_publisher_rule.by_family.rapids_anomalies.meets_rule; artifacts/findings/latest.json#dft_publisher_rule.by_family.rapids_anomalies.accepted --> Of the anomalies rows that do not meet the rule, 9,258 are the fast-charger events the revision moved to the fasts publication (they survive as duplicates of fasts rows) and 17,679 are accepted here with no stated reason for their exclusion. <!-- cite: artifacts/findings/latest.json#dft_publisher_rule.rapids_anomalies_not_meeting_rule.same_event_in_fasts_raw; artifacts/findings/latest.json#dft_publisher_rule.rapids_anomalies_not_meeting_rule.accepted_unexplained --> In the other direction, 274 rows of the rapids raw file meet the exclusion rule yet were published as clean. <!-- cite: artifacts/findings/latest.json#dft_publisher_rule.by_family.rapids.meets_rule -->

**Why it matters.** Anyone who reproduces the publisher's figures from the raw file and the stated rule will not get the publisher's numbers, and anyone who trusts the anomalies file as 'the bad rows' will discard thousands of ordinary sessions.

**What I would tell the decision-maker.** Treat the publisher's split as a label, not a rule: keep every row, carry the label as a flag, and apply one explicit session rule of your own (here: energy above zero and more than three minutes) so the exclusion is reproducible.

**What would change my mind.** A published methodology note listing the other exclusion criteria (incomplete records, events from other schemes) would explain the unexplained rows; the reports mention such criteria without counts.

**Why it matters.** A utilization figure quoted without its denominator definition is not comparable with any other, and the swing here is larger than most year-on-year changes an operator would act on.

**What I would tell the decision-maker.** Ask for the port-count method and the over-100% count with every utilization figure; treat the inferred figures as an upper bound on utilization (ports are a lower bound) and use the spread across definitions as the error bar.

**What would change my mind.** An inventory of ports per station would replace the inference and collapse the range.
